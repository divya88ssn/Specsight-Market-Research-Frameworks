## The one thing to hold onto

The research is **not** a march toward becoming Agile experts, building a Product
Operating System framework, or even cataloguing PM pain points. Those are *intermediate
outputs*. The destination has been constant since Document 1:

> **Operationalize the GTM Collaboration Proposal into a repeatable, evidence-driven GTM
> engine for Specsight** — a system that continuously discovers, validates, and
> operationalizes customer decision-making so that GTM, positioning, product strategy,
> and roadmap all improve together.

Read this way, the nine documents are a series of **increasingly constrained research
questions**. Each one narrows the search space while preserving that single objective.
The arc runs through three phases:

| Phase | Documents | Question the phase answers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1 · Build the Customer Decision Framework** | 1–2 | *What should we observe?* |
| **2 · Ground the Framework in Industry Reality** | 3–7 | *Are these real workflows prospects already recognize, and what do they call them?* |
| **3 · Connect Concepts into an Operating Model** | 8–9 | *How do mature organizations connect these concepts throughout delivery?* |

## Phase 1 — Building the Customer Decision Framework (Documents 1–2)

### Document 1 — Customer Decision Making Research Collaboration Proposal

- **Primary objective:** Move Specsight's GTM away from feature marketing toward
  understanding *how prospective customers make buying decisions*.
- **Key contribution:** Established the research operating model —
  *Customer Decision Research → Positioning → GTM Optimization → Product Strategy* — and
  introduced the **Commercial Learning Loop**, in which every customer interaction feeds
  back into positioning, product strategy, and GTM instead of those being run as separate
  functions.
- **How it advanced the research:** Defined **what** needed to be understood, but not
  **where** customer buying intent originates.
- **Gap it identified → next document:** *Where in a PM's daily workflow do buying
  triggers actually emerge?*

### Document 2 — Persona · JTBD · ICP · Buying Trigger Analysis  ★ Keystone

Document 2 is the **keystone of the entire body of work**. Everything before it defines
*why* the research is happening; everything after it exists to validate, refine, and
operationalize the frameworks it introduced using authentic industrial processes,
terminology, artifacts, and practitioner language. It was mistakenly treated at first as
"an ICP document" — it is really the **intellectual bridge** between the original GTM
proposal and all later workflow research.

- **Primary objective:** Convert customer decision research into an operational GTM
  framework rooted in PM workflows rather than personas or demographics.
- **Key contribution — six reusable frameworks that became the methodology for all
  subsequent research:**

  1. **PM Workflow → Emerging Failure Modes.** Not "What are PM pain points?" but "At each
     stage of the PM lifecycle, what *new* failure modes are emerging?"
  2. **Failure Mode → Missing Capability.** Translate every failure mode into a missing
     capability. Not *"poor requirements"* but *"missing continuous alignment capability."*
  3. **Missing Capability → Specsight Capability.** Reframe feature mapping as
     *Workflow problem → Capability gap → Specsight capability → Customer value.*
  4. **Workflow Event → Buying Trigger** (the biggest contribution). Reframe buying intent
     from *Company size → Budget → Industry* to
     *Operational event → Workflow breakdown → Recognition of capability gap → Buying trigger.*
  5. **Expert Best Practices vs Practitioner Pain.** Rather than studying frameworks, study
     *where practitioners complain* — this became the methodology for all remaining research.
  6. **PLG through Workflow Assessment.** Not "Try our product" but "Assess your workflow,"
     where the assessment itself becomes lead generation, qualification, education, and
     product demonstration.

- **How it advanced the research:** It produced the framework, but the frameworks were
  still Specsight's own abstractions.
- **Gap it identified → next phase:** *Do mature organizations actually work this way, and
  what terminology do they use?*

> **Why the later documents keep getting narrower:** Document 2 gave the *framework*. Every
> document after it is *evidence collection* against that framework — first "What workflow
> stages actually exist?", then "What do practitioners call them?", then "How do Microsoft,
> GitLab, and Atlassian operationalize them?", then "Where do practitioners complain those
> workflows break down?"

## Phase 2 — Grounding the Framework in Industry Reality (Documents 3–7)

The danger after Document 2 was that the frameworks could simply mirror Specsight's own
thinking. Phase 2 tests them against real industry practice and replaces internal
abstractions with the vocabulary practitioners already use.

### Document 3 — Implementation Validation Research

- **Primary objective:** Validate whether the hypothesized workflow stage called
  "Implementation Validation" actually exists in industry.
- **Key contribution:** Discovered practitioners don't generally call it Implementation
  Validation. The work is distributed across **Three Amigos, Story Clarification,
  Continuous Collaboration, Product Acceptance, and Sprint Review.** Implementation
  validation is an *umbrella concept*, not a formal process.
- **How it advanced the research:** Reframed a single hypothesized stage as a set of
  recognized industry activities.
- **Gap it identified → next document:** *What do Scrum and major organizations officially
  recommend?*

### Document 4 — Industry Validation of Continuous Product Collaboration

- **Primary objective:** Compare Document 3's findings against Scrum.org, Atlassian, and
  practitioner guidance.
- **Key contribution:** Validated that **continuous Product Owner collaboration, ongoing
  refinement, and incremental review** are widely recommended. Confirmed that
  **Implementation Walkthrough is not a Scrum ceremony** — it is simply one organizational
  implementation of continuous collaboration.
- **How it advanced the research:** Anchored the workflow in officially recommended practice.
- **Gap it identified → next document:** *What concepts do practitioners actually use most
  often?* — the terminology still wasn't complete.

### Document 5 — Industry Terminology Mapping

- **Primary objective:** Map the research into actual industry language.
- **Key contribution:** The point where the terminology shifted from invented names to
  adopted industry concepts — **Backlog Refinement, Three Amigos, Story Clarification,
  Acceptance Criteria, Definition of Done, Product Owner Acceptance, Continuous
  Collaboration.** Also identified which practitioners consistently publish on these topics
  and where those conversations occur.
- **How it advanced the research:** With the vocabulary settled, each concept could now be
  researched independently.
- **Gap it identified → next document:** The first branch chosen for deep study —
  *Acceptance Criteria → Definition of Done.*

### Document 6 — Product Operating System: Acceptance Criteria & Definition of Done (Part 1)

- **Primary objective:** Understand how mature engineering organizations operationalize
  Acceptance Criteria and Definition of Done.
- **Key contribution:** Compared **Microsoft, GitLab, Atlassian, and the UK National
  Archives.** Key findings: Acceptance Criteria define *"What should the feature do?"*;
  Definition of Done defines *"Is the increment ready to ship?"*; Product Owner acceptance
  is generally **embedded within Definition of Done** rather than existing as a separate
  workflow. Also catalogued the **evidence** used for different product types — consumer
  products, APIs, infrastructure, and AI products.
- **How it advanced the research:** Shifted the work from documentation toward operational
  evidence.
- **Gap it identified → next document:** *How do practitioners struggle with these concepts?*

### Document 7 — Product Operating System: Practitioner Failure Modes

- **Primary objective:** Study practitioner discussions rather than framework documentation.
- **Key contribution:** Instead of "How should Acceptance Criteria be written?", the
  research asked "Where does this workflow fail?" Recurring themes: ambiguous Acceptance
  Criteria, subjective Product Owner approval, missing evidence, late validation, unclear
  ownership, demo difficulties, and QA–Product–Engineering misalignment. The pivotal
  observation was that practitioners consistently discuss **operational symptoms** while
  almost nobody frames the larger problem as a cohesive validation capability — a far richer
  basis for buying triggers than generic "PM pain points."
- **How it advanced the research:** Established the empirical basis for the downstream
  Failure Mode Library (sibling parent DIV-33).
- **Gap it identified → next phase:** Individual concepts are understood; *how do they
  connect end-to-end?*

> **Scope boundary:** Document 7's specific failure modes belong to the Failure Mode Library
> (DIV-33). Here they are named only to explain why Phase 2 ends and Phase 3 begins.

## Phase 3 — Connecting Concepts into an Operating Model (Documents 8–9)

Documents 8–9 step back from individual artifacts and ask a higher-order question:
**how do all these concepts connect into an end-to-end Product Operating Model?** They no
longer explore individual artifacts — they explore the *relationships* between them.

### Document 8 — Discovery, Refinement and Verification Adjustment (Part 1)

- **Primary objective:** Determine how mature product organizations connect discovery,
  refinement, implementation, and acceptance into one continuous operating model.
- **Key contributions:**
  1. **Separation of two distinct product loops** — a Discovery/Refinement loop ("What
     should we build?") and a Verification/Acceptance loop ("Did we build what we
     agreed?"). *(Detailed in Section 3.)*
  2. **Clarification vs Requirement Evolution** — clarification improves shared
     understanding without changing Acceptance Criteria; requirement evolution introduces
     new business behavior and creates new backlog work. *(Detailed in Section 3.)*
  3. **Continuous Product Alignment (CPA)** as the higher-level operating model — Backlog
     Refinement, Product Validation, Acceptance, and Story Clarification are observable
     manifestations of CPA, not standalone practices. *(Detailed in Section 3.)*
  4. **Product Operating Model Research Framework** — study requirement evolution, PM
     involvement, engineering autonomy, customer validation, reviews, documentation, and
     communication across Microsoft, Stripe, Atlassian, GitHub, Shopify, and Amazon.
- **How it advanced the research:** Introduced a new level of abstraction — the operating
  *model* connecting the components, not the components themselves.
- **Gap it identified → next document:** Most published frameworks stop at
  *Discovery → PRD → Backlog → Sprint Review → Release*; the operational layer connecting
  these stages remains under-documented. *(This becomes the refinement boundary gap in
  Section 5.)*

### Document 9 — Discovery & Validation (Part 2)

- **Primary objective:** Build a research corpus around practitioners and thought leaders to
  validate whether Continuous Product Alignment is reflected in current industry thinking.
- **Key contributions:**
  1. **Curated research corpus** — a focused set of authors (Mike Cohn, Joel
     Bancroft-Connors, Maarten Dalmijn, Roman Pichler, Barry Overeem, Stefan Wolpers, Lenny
     Rachitsky, Shreyas Doshi, Saurabh Juneja), each representing a distinct aspect of the
     Product Operating Model. *(Catalogued in Section 3.)*
  2. **Practitioner corpus over frameworks** — practitioners discussing real sprint failures
     (implementation drift, skipped refinement, unclear acceptance criteria, blocked
     engineering, velocity loss, developers interpreting stories differently) provide richer
     signals than well-known Agile guidance.
  3. **Refinement Boundary** — nearly everyone writes about *Vision → Backlog Refinement →
     Ready for Development*; very few describe *Ready for Development → Implementation →
     Product Validation → Sprint Review*. The gap is not about *preparing* work — it is about
     *maintaining product intent during execution* and validating it before stakeholder
     review. *(Analyzed in full in Section 5.)*
  4. **Emerging schools of practice** — Traditional Scrum vs AI-native / Product-led, with
     the research proposing to study *where organizations draw the boundary between
     clarification and new requirements*. *(Detailed in Section 3.)*
- **How it advanced the research:** Integrated the earlier Product OS work into a single
  operating-model view and named the frontier for future research.

## The end-to-end engine this narrative is building

Every document contributes another layer to a single pipeline. Note that **nothing here
starts with Specsight** — everything starts with *the customer's operating model*, because
**buying triggers emerge from operating systems**, not from demographics, company size, or
job titles.

```text
Customer Decision Research
        │
        ▼
PM Workflow
        │
        ▼
Industry Operating Model
        │
        ▼
Observable Workflow Events
        │
        ▼
Emerging Failure Modes
        │
        ▼
Capability Gaps
        │
        ▼
Buying Triggers
        │
        ▼
ICP Refinement
        │
        ▼
PLG Workflow Assessment
        │
        ▼
Messaging
        │
        ▼
Demo
        │
        ▼
Product Feedback
        │
        ▼
Roadmap
```

This is why the research keeps returning to Product Operating Systems: not because Specsight
sells one, but because understanding the workflow, decisions, artifacts, collaboration
patterns, terminology, and failure modes is what lets Specsight identify *when* someone is
likely to experience friction, *how* they describe it, *what* workaround they use, *what*
capability they are missing, and *whether Specsight is a natural fit*.
